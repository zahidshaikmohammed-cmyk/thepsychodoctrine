# Stage C — Pilot: Weapons 1–10, expanded

Each weapon keeps its original doctrine lines unchanged. A new **Rebuttal** section follows them: the client's exact words, then the full spoken response. `[pause]` marks a deliberate beat. Square brackets such as `[Firm]` are placeholders for facts only you can fill in (firm name, regulator, registration number, fee structure).

This file is the source the book build reads, so edits here flow straight into the PDF.

## 1 — The Fireline of Origin
**Client:** “Who is this? How did you get my number?”

Fair question — and you should ask it on every call like this one. My name is [Name], I'm with [Firm], registered with [regulator] under [registration number]. Your number came from [source — the enquiry you left on our site, the webinar list you joined]. If that's wrong, tell me now and I'll remove it today.

[pause] I'm calling for one reason. Most people who trade on their own are paying more in costs and carrying more risk than they realise, and they only find out when a bad month exposes it.

I'm not going to pitch you a stock. I don't yet know whether we can help you — that's what the next sixty seconds decide. If what I say isn't relevant, you hang up and you never hear from me again. That's the deal.

You won't remember this call because I was loud. You'll remember it because I was straight with you. Can I have one minute?

## 2 — The Reason for War
**Client:** “What's in it for you?”

Straight answer: I get paid. [pause] [Firm] pays me [state the real structure — salary plus commission on brokerage, or fixed salary]. So yes, I benefit when you open an account and trade. You should know that before you believe a single word I say.

Here's why that incentive doesn't have to work against you. A client who blows up their account in three months leaves — and tells five friends. A client who trades sensibly stays for ten years. The only version of this job that pays me over time is the one where you're still here, still solvent, still trading. That's not charity. It's arithmetic.

And if anything I recommend ever looks built for my commission instead of your outcome, make me justify it in writing. That's your right, and I'll honour it.

So let me ask the only question that matters on this call: what are you trying to make your money do over the next three years?

## 3 — The Vibration Structure
**Client:** “Just send me an email.”

I'll send it — today. [pause] But let me make sure it's worth opening, because you get forty of these a week and delete thirty-nine. Give me ninety seconds and I'll tell you exactly what will be in it.

Three things. One: what you would actually pay here, per trade and per month, based on how you trade now. Two: what happens to your money when you're wrong — the margin rules and the worst case, in plain numbers. Three: who you'd be dealing with, and how to verify us before you trust us with a single rupee.

If those three answers don't beat what you have now, delete it and we're done — no follow-up. If they do, you'll have questions, and an email can't answer questions.

So: it lands in your inbox within the hour, you read it tonight, and I call you on [Thursday at six] for ten minutes. Does Thursday at six work, or is Friday morning better?

## 4 — The Final Push
**Client:** “Call me next month.”

I can do that. Before I do, one honest question: what changes next month? [pause]

If there's a real reason — a bonus landing, a loan closing, exams, a move — then next month is the right answer, and I'll call on the day you choose. No games.

But if nothing changes, next month quietly becomes the month after, and the month after that. That isn't a decision. That's drift. I'm not going to invent a deadline for you — there isn't one. Accounts don't close to new clients next month. What does happen is that every month you wait, you're choosing by default instead of on purpose.

So let's make it on purpose. Either we settle the one question that's genuinely holding you back, right now, or we put a specific date in the calendar along with the specific thing you'll have decided by then. Which is it — the question, or the date?

## 5 — The First Strike
**Client:** “You've got thirty seconds.”

Thirty seconds. Starting now. [pause]

Most active traders I speak to have never added up what they paid last year in brokerage, charges and slippage combined. When they finally see it on one page, the number stops them cold. I can build that page for you from a single contract note — not a projection, your actual trades. If I can't show you a real saving, or a real risk you're not managing, I'll say so and you'll never hear from me again.

That's my thirty. [pause]

Now it's your move: five more minutes to see your own number, or I let you go right now, no hard feelings.

## 6 — The Entry Trigger
**Client:** “I already have a broker.”

Good. Most serious people do — I'd be worried if you didn't. [pause]

I'm not calling to ask you to leave them. I'm calling because most people chose their broker once, years ago, and never checked again. Pricing changed, tools changed, the market changed. The account didn't.

So three quick questions. What are you paying per trade, all-in, including charges? When the market moved hard last [month], could you actually get through — app, phone, anyone? And when you have a question, does anyone there know your account?

If all three answers make you happy, stay where you are. I mean that. If even one of them made you hesitate, that hesitation is worth ten minutes.

Send me one recent contract note — just one — and I'll put your current cost next to ours, line by line. If yours wins, I'll tell you so. Fair?

## 7 — The Breath of Confidence
**Client:** “How do you know this stock is going up?”

I don't. [pause]

And anyone who tells you they know where a stock will be next week is either lying or about to learn something expensive.

Here's what I do know — and this is where my confidence lives. I know how much you can lose on a trade before you place it. I know where the exit sits and why it sits there. I know what share of your account is at risk at any moment, and I keep it small enough that one bad call can't end you.

Prediction is a coin toss with better vocabulary. Process is what keeps you in the game long enough for your good calls to matter.

So I won't sell you a forecast. I'll show you how a trade is actually built — entry, exit, size, worst case — and you judge whether that process deserves your money. Want me to walk you through one, start to finish?

## 8 — The Focus Lock
**Client:** “Look — the market's crazy right now, I lost money last year, my wife handles our finances, and honestly I don't have the time.”

That's four things, and every one of them is real. [pause]

Let's not try to solve all four at once — that's how conversations go in circles and nobody decides anything. So let me ask you this: if one of those four disappeared tomorrow, which one would actually change your answer? [pause — let them answer]

Okay. Then that's the one we deal with first.

The others still matter. Time has a straightforward answer, and I'll give it to you. And if your wife runs the finances, she shouldn't be handed a decision after the fact — she should be on the next call, asking her own questions.

But the volatile market and last year's loss are really one fear wearing two coats: will this hurt me again? That deserves a direct answer, not a pep talk.

So tell me about last year. What did you buy, how much of your money was in it, and what made you sell?

## 9 — The 3-Second Kill Zone
**Client:** “Leverage? That's just gambling.”

You're right to be suspicious. Leverage is where most retail traders get destroyed. [pause]

Let me show you the maths, because the maths is the whole story. At five times leverage, a 2% move in your favour is a 10% gain on your money. A 2% move against you is a 10% loss. A 20% move against you wipes the position out. It cuts both ways, at exactly the same speed.

And the record isn't kind. SEBI's own study found that 93% of individual F&O traders in India lost money between FY22 and FY24 — an average loss of roughly two lakh rupees each, including costs. That isn't my opinion. That's the regulator's data.

So here's where I stand. Leverage isn't for everyone, and it is never for money you can't afford to lose. You can run this account with no leverage at all. If you use it, it's small, with a hard stop, sized so a bad day is a bruise — not a funeral.

One question before anything else: if this position went to zero, would it change how you live next month?

## 10 — The Client Fracture
**Client:** “I lost money in the market before. Never again.”

I hear you. And I won't pretend that loss didn't matter — it did. [pause]

Can I ask what happened? Not to judge it. To understand it.

In my experience, losses usually come from one of four places: a tip from someone with no accountability, a position too big for the account, no exit plan, or plain bad timing on a sound idea. The first three are fixable. The fourth happens to everyone, and the only defence against it is size.

If we look at that trade together and the problem turns out to be the product, or the market itself, then staying out may genuinely be the right call for you — and I'll tell you so. But if it was one of the fixable three, then "never again" is protecting you from a mistake, not from the market. Those are very different things.

Walk me through it. What did you buy, how much of your money was in it, and what made you get out?
