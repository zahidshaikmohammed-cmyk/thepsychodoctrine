# Rebuttals — Weapons 81–125

## 81 — The Invisible Contract
**Client:** “Is there fine print I should know about?”

This is the decision that will shape your future. [pause] So yes — let's read the fine print together, line by line, before you sign anything.

The fine print is where the real contract lives. Not the brochure, not this call. Here's what's in it. [Fees: what, when, how much.] [Lock-in or notice periods, if any.] [What happens if your account value falls below a certain level — including whether positions can be closed without your say.] [How and when you can withdraw.] [What the firm is and isn't responsible for.]

I'll walk you through each one. Then I'll send it to you to read alone, in your own time. Anything you don't understand, mark it, and we'll go through it again. Anything you don't like, tell me — some things can be adjusted, some can't, and I'll be honest about which is which.

No one should sign a contract they couldn't explain to someone else.

Shall we start with the section most people skip — what happens on a really bad day?

## 82 — The Final Turning Point
**Client:** “I'm happy with how things are.”

This is the moment that defines your future. [pause] And if you're genuinely happy, then the right future might be the one you've already got.

So let me test it — not to talk you out of it, but to make sure "happy" isn't just "used to it."

Is your money doing what you need it to do? Not "is it safe" — is it growing faster than your costs rise? If your income stopped, how long would your savings last? Are you on track for the things that matter — retirement, kids, freedom? And when you think about your finances, is the feeling contentment, or avoidance?

If all of that checks out, then you don't need me, and I'll say so. Contentment backed by numbers is rare. Respect it.

If one of those questions made you hesitate, that's where your turning point is.

Did one of them land?

## 83 — The Decision-Making Symphony
**Client:** “This is all too complicated for me.”

This decision is part of your transformation. [pause] And complicated is my fault, not yours. Let me strip it down.

Every investment, no matter how fancy it sounds, comes down to three moving parts. One: what you put in. Two: what it's exposed to — what makes it go up or down. Three: how you get out.

Here, that's: [amount] going into [product], which moves with [market/driver], and you can exit by [process]. That's the whole symphony. The rest is detail — important detail, but detail you can learn one piece at a time.

Complexity is often used to make people feel they need an expert. Sometimes they do. But you should never invest in something you can't explain in three sentences.

So try it. Three parts — what you put in, what moves it, how you get out. Can you say it back to me?

## 84 — The Guilt of Inaction
**Client:** “I've been meaning to start investing for years.”

What will you regret more — saying yes, or saying no? [pause] That's the question you've been carrying for years, and it's heavy because you've never put it down.

"Meaning to" is a strange place to live. You're not deciding against it — you'd have dropped it by now. You're not deciding for it — you'd have started. You're in the middle, and the middle has a cost: the guilt every time you think about it, and the time that keeps passing.

So let's look at why it hasn't happened. Was it not knowing where to start? Fear of losing? Never having a spare moment? Not trusting anyone to help?

Each of those has a different solution. None of them requires a big leap. Usually the thing that's been stopping you is smaller than the years it's cost.

Which one has been holding you back?

## 85 — The Final Shift
**Client:** “I'd rather stick with gold and property. I understand those.”

This decision will change everything. Are you ready? [pause] Not because you should abandon gold or property. Because "everything in two places" is its own kind of risk.

Gold and property are solid. You can touch them, and they've protected families for generations. I understand completely why you trust them.

But understand what each one gives you and doesn't. Property is hard to sell quickly, concentrated in one location, and expensive to buy and sell. Gold protects against certain shocks but produces no income. Both can go through long stretches of going nowhere.

The point of adding market investments isn't to replace them. It's to stop your entire future depending on two assets behaving well at the same time.

So let's look at the whole picture. What share of your wealth is in gold and property right now — roughly?

## 86 — The Divine Tension
**Client:** “I feel uncomfortable about this.”

The more you resist, the stronger the force grows. [pause] But let's be careful — sometimes discomfort is resistance, and sometimes it's wisdom. I don't know which yours is yet, and neither do you.

So let's take it apart. Is the discomfort about the money — the amount feels too big? About the risk — you're not sure you understand what could go wrong? About me — something I've said doesn't sit right? Or about change — doing something you've never done before?

If it's the amount, we lower it. If it's the risk, we go through it again until it's clear. If it's me, tell me — I'd rather hear it. If it's change, that's normal, and it tends to fade once you've taken a small step.

Discomfort is information. It's just not always telling you to stop.

Which kind is it?

## 87 — The Finality of Yes
**Client:** “Fine. Okay. Sign me up.”

This is your decision. Welcome to the future. [pause] But hold on — that "fine" sounded more like surrender than conviction, and I don't want your surrender.

A yes that comes out of tiredness, or just to end the conversation, turns into doubt by tomorrow and regret by next week. Then you'll associate this decision with being worn down, and the first bad day, you'll want out.

So let's check it. Tell me, in one sentence, why you're saying yes. Not why I think you should — why you think so.

If you've got a reason — a real one — we go ahead, and I'm glad. If you can't name one, then let's not do this today. Sleep on it. Call me tomorrow if it still makes sense. I'll still be here.

So — your reason?

## 88 — The Unseen Control
**Client:** “I want to see what results other clients have got.”

You don't need to see it to believe it. [pause] Actually, you should be very careful about believing what you're shown.

Client results are the easiest thing in the world to cherry-pick. Every firm can find a client who did brilliantly. Screenshots can be selected, timeframes chosen to flatter. Testimonials tell you about one person's luck, not about what's likely to happen to you.

What matters more is this. Are performance figures presented fairly — including the bad periods, after costs, compared with a simple benchmark? Is the firm regulated, and has it ever been penalised? Can you speak to the process, not just the outcomes?

[If the firm publishes verified performance or regulated disclosures, point to them here.]

I'll show you what I can, honestly, with the losses alongside the gains. But the best evidence is your own: start small, watch how it's managed, and judge from there.

What would you count as convincing evidence?

## 89 — The External Pressure
**Client:** “My colleagues all lost money in the market last year.”

The decision isn't for tomorrow. It's now. [pause] Because what happened to your colleagues is worth studying today — before you make any decision.

So let's look at it. Do you know what they did? Most people who lose money in a bad year make one of a few mistakes. They bought on tips without understanding what they owned. They put in money they needed soon. They used borrowed money or leverage. They panicked and sold at the bottom. Or they traded too often, chasing every move.

Losses in a falling market are normal — prices fall, and portfolios fall with them. Losses that wreck people come from those mistakes.

Their experience isn't a reason to stay out. It's a map of what not to do.

So ask them — what did they actually buy, and why did they sell? Then let's make sure your plan avoids every one of those traps. What did they do, as far as you know?

## 90 — The Mind Bender
**Client:** “I'll just follow the tips on YouTube and Telegram.”

You've already decided. Now make it happen. [pause] But make sure you know who you've decided to follow.

Here's the question nobody asks about tips: how does the person giving them make money? If they earn from subscriptions, views or paid groups, then their income comes from your attention, not your returns. Some of them are honest. Many aren't registered or accountable to anyone, and some profit by getting followers to buy what they already own.

When their tip goes wrong, there's no one to call, nothing to claim, no responsibility. You absorb the loss alone.

I'm not saying never learn from public content. Learn all you can. But a tip is a conclusion without a process. If you don't understand why you're buying, you won't know when to sell.

So test it. Take their last five calls. Would you have made money — after costs — if you'd followed every one?

## 91 — The Unseen Force
**Client:** “The market's rigged. The big players control everything.”

You don't need to see it to believe it. [pause] And you're not entirely wrong — which is why I won't dismiss you.

Big institutions do have advantages: better information systems, faster execution, lower costs. Manipulation does happen, and people do get caught and penalised. Your scepticism is earned.

But "rigged" implies you can't win, and that's where the logic breaks. If you try to out-trade the big players on short-term moves, you're on their turf, and yes, the odds are against you. If you own good businesses for years, their speed advantage barely matters. The returns of a company over a decade come from its profits, not from who traded it fastest this afternoon.

And there is a regulator watching — surveillance systems, penalties, enforcement. Imperfect, but real.

So what would it take to change your mind — what evidence would convince you that a long-term investor can still do fine?

## 92 — The Finishing Move
**Client:** “Okay. So what exactly do I do now?”

You've made your choice. There's no going back. [pause] Well — you can always go back. But let's make sure you don't need to. Here's exactly what happens, step by step.

Step one: [KYC/account opening] — about [time], you'll need [documents]. Step two: we confirm your plan in writing — amount, risk limit, review date. Step three: you fund the account through [official channel only — never to a personal account]. Step four: we place your first position together, and I'll explain every click. Step five: we review on [date], whatever's happened.

That's it. No hidden steps, no surprise costs.

Write those five down, or I'll email them to you right now. If anything goes wrong at any step, you call me.

Shall we start step one while we're on the phone?

## 93 — The Unstoppable Shift
**Client:** “I've never invested anything. I'm a complete beginner.”

This is not just a sale. It's a transformation. [pause] And being a beginner is a strength, not a weakness. You've got no bad habits yet.

Here's how beginners should start, and it's not what most people do. Most beginners jump straight in with real money because a friend made a profit. Then the first loss frightens them out for years.

So we go the other way. First, you learn the basics — what you're buying, what moves it, what can go wrong. [If available: a demo or practice account, so you can feel the swings without paying for them.] Then you start real, but small — an amount you're truly okay losing, so the lessons are cheap. Then, only once you understand how you react to a falling market, do you think about more.

That's not slow. That's how you last.

Would you like to start with practice first, or a small real amount?

## 94 — The Psycho Evolution Point
**Client:** “Will this actually change anything for me?”

You will not return to this version once you say yes. [pause] But I want to be honest about what changes, because it isn't what most people think.

Your bank balance won't transform next month. If anyone promises that, run. What changes is more fundamental, and it happens faster. You'll have a plan where there was a vague worry. You'll understand where your money is and why. You'll make decisions on purpose instead of by default. And over years — if you stay consistent — the money follows the habits.

The version you won't return to is the one who doesn't know. Once you understand how your money works, you can't un-know it. That's the evolution.

If what you need is a fast change in your circumstances, this won't do it, and I won't pretend it will.

So which do you need — fast change, or lasting change?

## 95 — The Internal Snap
**Client:** “I'm holding onto my losing stocks. They'll come back eventually.”

You're holding on to the version of you that dies today. [pause] Not literally. I mean the version who waits for the market to undo a decision instead of facing it.

Here's the uncomfortable question. If you had that money in cash right now, would you buy those exact stocks today, at today's price? If yes, keep them — you still believe in them, and that's a valid position. If no, then you're not investing. You're waiting to get your money back, and the market doesn't know or care what you paid.

That's the sunk cost trap. The original price is history. The only question is where the money has the best chance from here.

Sometimes the answer is hold. Sometimes it's cut and redeploy. But it has to be a fresh decision, not hope.

Want to go through them one by one with that question?

## 96 — The Final Ego Slap
**Client:** “I don't need anyone's help. I can manage myself.”

This ego is the reason you're stuck. [pause] That's harsh, and I might be wrong. So let me explain what I mean before you hang up.

I'm not against you — I'm against your cage. Doing it yourself is a strength. Independence is good. But independence becomes a cage when it means you never let anyone check your work. Every professional — surgeons, pilots, top traders — has someone reviewing them. Not because they're weak. Because nobody can see their own blind spots.

So here's a test. Has your approach delivered what you wanted over the last three years? Not "made money" — done what you needed? If yes, you don't need me, genuinely. If no, the question is whether you're managing well or just managing alone.

What would your results look like if someone had been checking your blind spots?

## 97 — The Gutline
**Client:** “I'll start when I have more money saved up.”

Every day you delay, your future shrinks. [pause] Maybe. Or maybe waiting is right. Let's find out which.

There's one good reason to wait: you don't have an emergency fund. If you don't have three to six months of expenses set aside, build that first. Investing money you might need next month forces you to sell at the worst time. That's not waiting — that's sequencing, and it's smart.

But if your emergency fund is there, "more money" is usually a moving target. When you have ₹1 lakh, you'll want ₹2 lakh. The amount never feels like enough, because the hesitation was never about the amount.

Starting small isn't pointless. It builds the habit, the knowledge, and the nerve — all of which matter more than the first figure.

So which is it — do you have the emergency fund in place?

## 98 — The Glass Smash
**Client:** “I don't really like looking at my finances.”

If you looked at your life like a window — what would you see? [pause] Most people who avoid their finances already suspect the answer, and that's why they don't look.

Avoidance makes complete sense. Looking means facing numbers that might not be what you'd hoped, and that feels bad. Not looking feels fine — until the day you have to.

Now let's break it. Not your life — the avoidance. Because what you imagine is almost always worse than what's actually there. And even if it's bad, a bad number you know is a problem you can fix. A bad number you avoid just grows.

So here's a small, painless version. Ten minutes. Four numbers: what comes in each month, what goes out, what you own, what you owe. No judgment. Just a snapshot.

Can we do those ten minutes now?

## 99 — The Future Collapse
**Client:** “Retirement's a long way off. I'll think about it later.”

This is the future knocking. Will you answer or ignore it again? [pause] Because "a long way off" is exactly why it matters now.

Here's how compounding treats time. Money invested in your twenties or thirties has decades to grow. The same amount started in your forties has far less time to work, so you need to put away much more each month to reach the same place. The cost of starting late isn't linear — it climbs.

I'm not going to quote you returns, because nobody knows them. But the principle holds in any market: time is the most powerful input you have, and it's the only one you can't top up later.

You don't have to solve retirement today. You just have to start the clock.

So let's work out one number: what monthly amount could you put away without feeling it?

## 100 — The Reflective Burn
**Client:** “I'll definitely start soon. I mean it this time.”

How long will you keep telling yourself that? [pause] I'm not mocking you. I'm asking because I think you'd want me to.

"Soon" is one of the most comfortable words there is. It lets you feel committed without doing anything. And I suspect you've said it before — maybe more than once.

So let's check, honestly. When did you first say you'd start? What's changed since then? If the answer is "nothing much," then "soon" isn't a plan. It's a way of not deciding.

That's not a character flaw. It's what everyone does with things that feel big and uncertain. The fix isn't more willpower. It's making the first step so small that "soon" becomes "today."

What's the smallest step that would make this real — not the whole thing, just the first move?

## 101 — The Death of Option B
**Client:** “I like to keep my options open.”

There is no Option B. Only regression. [pause] That's too strong — let me say it properly.

Keeping options open is smart when you don't have enough information. But there's a point where it stops being flexibility and starts being avoidance. If you keep every door open, you never walk through any of them. You just stand in the corridor.

Backup plans are good. A primary plan is better. So let's sort them. What's your main plan for your money right now — the one you're actually committed to? If you can name it, great. Then this is either part of it, or it isn't.

If you can't name it, that's the real issue. Not this decision. The absence of any decision.

So — what's Plan A?

## 102 — The Self-Bet Script
**Client:** “I'm just not good with money.”

Every time you say no, you bet against yourself. [pause] And "I'm not good with money" is a bet you've been placing for years.

But it isn't a fact about you. It's a description of what's happened so far. Being "good with money" isn't a talent you're born with — it's a set of systems. Automate your savings so willpower isn't needed. Set rules in advance so decisions aren't made on impulse. Review regularly so problems are small when you catch them.

People who look "good with money" usually just have better systems, not better brains.

So let's stop betting against yourself and build one system today. Just one: an automatic transfer, on payday, of an amount you won't miss. You don't have to be good with money for that to work. You just have to set it up once.

What amount wouldn't you notice?

## 103 — The Pressure Mute
**Client:** “Can you give me some space to think?”

You already know what to do. I'm just waiting. [pause] And I'll wait properly — which means going quiet, not hovering.

Take the space. I'm not going to follow up tomorrow, or the day after. Here's what I'll do instead. I'll send you a short summary — what we discussed, the costs, the risks, the next step if you want it. Then I'll leave it alone.

When you're ready — whether that's a week or a month — you call me. If I haven't heard from you by [date], I'll send one message to check whether you want to continue or close it. One. Then nothing.

Space is how good decisions get made. Pressure is how bad ones do.

Is [date] a reasonable time for that one check-in, or would you prefer I didn't contact you at all?

## 104 — The Iron Script
**Client:** “Can you put all of that in writing?”

These words are not sales. They are law. [pause] Or they should be — which is exactly why you're right to want them in writing.

Anything I've said on this call that matters to your decision should be on paper. The product, the costs, the risks, how you exit, what I've committed to. If I said it, I'll write it. If I'm not willing to write it, you shouldn't believe it.

And here's my promise: if anything in the written version differs from what I told you, the written version wins, and you should challenge me on the difference.

I'll send it within [timeframe]. Read it without me. Mark anything that's unclear or doesn't match what you heard. Then we'll go through your marks together.

Words fly away. Paper stays. What email should I send it to?

## 105 — The Unheard Close
**Client:** “Sorry, can you go over that again?”

I'm going to say this once. If you miss it, it's gone. [pause] That's what a pressure seller would say. I'll say it as many times as you need — but this time I'll say it better.

Here it is, in four lines. What it is: [product]. What you put in: [amount]. What can happen: it can go up, and it can go down — you could lose part or all of it. How you get out: [exit process].

That's the core. Everything else is detail.

And I'll put those four lines in writing right after this call, so you never have to rely on memory. If I've explained it badly the first time, that's on me, not you.

So, those four lines — which one would you like me to unpack?

## 106 — The Future Funeral
**Client:** “I'll just wait and see what happens.”

We're not closing a deal. We're burying the version of you that waits. [pause] Or not — if waiting is actually the right call. Let's work out which.

"Wait and see" sounds neutral, but it's still a decision. It's a decision to let events choose for you. Sometimes that's wise: when you genuinely lack information, or when something specific is about to become clear. Sometimes it's just a way of avoiding the discomfort of committing.

So, what exactly are you waiting to see? Name it. A price level? A news event? A change in your own finances?

If you can name it, we set a trigger: when that happens, you decide. If you can't name it, then you're not waiting for anything. You're just waiting.

What's the thing you're waiting to see?

## 107 — The Deep Pull
**Client:** “I used to take risks. These days I play it safe.”

I know the version of you that was bold. Where is it? [pause] And maybe the answer is: it grew up. That's not a bad thing.

Risk tolerance changes, and it should. If you've got a family now, a mortgage, people who depend on you, then playing it safer isn't fear. It's responsibility. I'd never push you to take risks your life can't absorb.

But there's a difference between safe and stuck. Safe means your risk matches your situation. Stuck means you stopped taking any risk at all after one bad experience, and your money's been standing still ever since.

So let's find out which. What made you shift? Was it a change in your circumstances — or a loss you never quite got over?

Want to wake up that boldness again — but sized to the life you have now?

## 108 — The Fog Cutter
**Client:** “I'm still a bit lost with all this.”

Let me make it clear — nothing changes unless you act. [pause] But acting while you're lost is worse than not acting at all. So let's clear the fog first.

You don't need to understand everything. You need to understand the one thing that's confusing you. Usually it's a single piece — how the money moves, what the risk really means, how fees work, how you get out — and once that piece clicks, the rest falls into place.

So don't tell me you're lost in general. Tell me where. Pick the part of what I've said that felt the haziest. Just one.

I'll explain it as simply as I can. If it's still not clear, I'll explain it a different way. We keep going until you could teach it to someone else.

Which part felt foggiest?

## 109 — The Body Trigger
**Client:** “Honestly, this is making me nervous.”

Feel that tightness in your chest? [pause] Listen to it. That's not your soul trying to break out — I'm not going to dress it up. It's your body telling you something matters.

Nervousness about money is useful. It means you take the risk seriously. The question is whether it's telling you this is wrong, or telling you this is new.

So let's check. When does the tightness get worse — when we talk about the amount, about the risk, or about committing? If it's the amount, it's probably too big. Let's halve it. If it's the risk, we haven't explained it clearly enough. If it's the commitment, that's just the feeling of doing something new, and it fades.

I'd rather you start small and calm than big and anxious. Anxious money makes bad decisions.

You're not afraid. You're paying attention. So — at what amount does the tightness ease?

## 110 — The Last Door
**Client:** “Can I just join later if I want to?”

This is the last door before you lose access. [pause] That's what a pressure seller would tell you. The truth is: yes, you can almost certainly join later.

[If there's a genuine limit — a closing date, a capacity cap — state it plainly here, with proof.]

So "later" isn't a door slamming. It's just later. And if later is when you'll be ready, then later is right.

The only door that really closes is time. Not this offer — your time. If this would genuinely help you, starting later means less time for it to work. If it wouldn't, joining later or never makes no difference.

So decide on merit, not fear. Forget any deadline. If this were available forever, would you want it — yes or no?

## 111 — The Suffering Upgrade
**Client:** “I'm already losing money on other investments. I can't take more.”

You're already paying the price. [pause] So the first thing we do isn't add anything. It's stop the bleeding.

Putting more money in while you're losing elsewhere is how people dig deeper. So before we talk about anything new, let's look at what you've got. What are you in? Why did you buy it? Has the reason changed, or just the price?

Some of those losses may be temporary — good assets in a bad patch. Hold those. Some may be mistakes — bought on a tip, too concentrated, not understood. Those might need cutting.

You've been suffering for free. If we're going to do anything, it has to turn that suffering into learning: understanding what went wrong so it doesn't happen again.

Only after that do we think about anything new. Shall we review what you're holding first?

## 112 — The Detachment Blade
**Client:** “I'll think about it.”

I don't sell to the version that needs to think. I speak to the one that acts. [pause] But thinking is fine. Let's just make sure the thinking has something to work on.

"I'll think about it" usually means one of three things. There's a specific question you haven't asked yet. There's someone you need to speak to. Or it's a polite no.

If it's a question, ask me now. If it's a person, tell me who, and I'll send what they need. If it's a polite no, just say no. I won't argue, and it saves us both time.

And if you genuinely want to think it over, let's give the thinking a shape. What exactly will you think about? The amount? The risk? Whether you trust me? Name the one or two things, and we set a time — [day] — when you'll have your answer.

What's the thing you'll be thinking about?

## 114 — The Psycho Pressure Loop
**Client:** “I'm still not sure. I don't know.”

You said time. That's sorted — we set a review date. You said money. That's handled — the amount is one you've said you can afford to lose. You said clarity. We crossed that — you explained it back to me. [pause]

So let's look at what's left. Because each of those was a real concern, and we've dealt with each one. If you're still not sure, then there's something we haven't named yet.

That's not a criticism. Sometimes the real reason sits under the reasons we say out loud. It might be trust. It might be a partner you haven't told. It might be that, deep down, you just don't want to — which is completely allowed.

So I'll ask once, and then I'll respect whatever you say. What's the thing that's still stopping you?

## 115 — Mental Collapse Strategy
**Client:** “I need more time.”

What has comfort ever created for you? [pause] That's a hard question, and I'm not asking it to push you. I'm asking because "I need time" can mean two very different things.

Sometimes it means: I need time to check my finances, talk to my spouse, read the documents. That's real, and you should take it. I'll give you a date and won't chase you before it.

Sometimes it means: I need the uncomfortable feeling to go away. And if that's the case, time won't help. You've needed time for years on decisions like this. Look where that got you — the same question, still open.

So which one is it? If you need time to do something specific, tell me what, and we set a date. If you need time for the feeling to pass, let's talk about the feeling instead.

What would you do with the time?

## 116 — The Noise Killer
**Client:** “Can you send me more information first?”

You don't need more info. You need to decide. [pause] Or maybe you do need more — but only one specific piece. Let's find it.

More information feels productive. In practice, most people who ask for more already have enough, and another brochure just becomes another thing not to read. The fog doesn't come from too little information. It comes from not knowing which piece matters.

So tell me: if you could know one more thing before deciding, what would it be? The worst-case scenario? How fees work? How fast you can withdraw? What happens if [firm] goes bust?

Whatever it is, I'll answer it now. If it needs a document, I'll send that one document — not a pack of twenty.

What's the one thing you'd need to know?

## 117 — The Fatal Mirror
**Client:** “Doing nothing is the safest option.”

What's the cost of staying the same? [pause] Say it again — out loud. Because doing nothing isn't free. It just sends the bill later.

You're right that doing nothing protects you from one kind of risk: losing money in the market. That's real. But it exposes you to others. Inflation eats savings left idle. Goals drift further out of reach when nothing's growing toward them. And if your one income ever stops, there's nothing working in the background.

Neither choice is risk-free. One risk is loud and visible: the market falls and you see it. The other is quiet and invisible: you never see the money you didn't make, or the slow loss of purchasing power.

So you're not choosing between risk and safety. You're choosing which risk you'd rather live with.

Now tell me what it feels like to break the pattern of doing nothing — even in a small way.

## 118 — The 7-Second War
**Client:** “Oh — you again.”

This call isn't for the version of you that hesitates. [pause] It's for the one who said, last time, that they'd look into it. Seven seconds — and if it's not a good time, I'll go.

Last time we spoke, you were interested in [topic] and wanted to check [specific thing]. Did you get the chance?

If yes — what did you find? If no, that's fine; life happens. But let's be honest with each other about where this is. Is it still something you want to look at? Or has it slipped down the list for good?

What's your pattern — escape, or rise? Both are allowed. I'd just rather know than keep calling someone who's already moved on.

So — still on the table, or should I take it off?

## 119 — The Last Version
